# Neural Networks Should Widen Opportunity, Not Close the Door

Neural networks should be used in high-impact admissions and hiring decisions only within a deliberately limited decision-support role. My dividing line is not simply whether a human remains somewhere in the process. It is whether the system can independently deny an opportunity. A model may help identify additional candidates for review, detect inconsistent evaluations, or estimate where an applicant may need support. It should not automatically reject a university applicant or remove a job candidate from consideration.

This distinction matters because the costs of prediction errors are asymmetric. A false positive might cause a reviewer to spend additional time examining a candidate who is ultimately not selected. A false negative can prevent a qualified person from receiving an education, interview, or career opportunity, and that person may never know an algorithm influenced the outcome. Conventional accuracy can conceal this difference because it counts both mistakes while ignoring their unequal human consequences. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) recommends evaluating AI risk according to both the likelihood and magnitude of potential impacts, rather than treating technical accuracy as the sole measure of trustworthiness (Tabassi, 2023).

I would therefore design the network as an opportunity-expansion system. For example, admissions officers could establish a group of applicants who clearly qualify under published criteria and then use the model to surface additional candidates whose potential may be obscured by nontraditional educational histories. In hiring, the model could search for transferable skills rather than rank every applicant from “best” to “worst.” This approach uses pattern recognition to challenge a narrow initial screen instead of automating exclusion.

Human review alone, however, is not an adequate safeguard. Reviewers can develop automation bias and accept a model’s recommendation without meaningful evaluation. NIST notes that human-AI interaction can sometimes amplify bias and recommends clearly assigning responsibility for decisions and oversight (Tabassi, 2023). A defensible process would consequently require subgroup-specific false-negative testing, documented model limitations, periodic drift monitoring, and an appeal process through which applicants can correct data or request reconsideration. Reviewers should also see relevant evidence and uncertainty, not a single authoritative-looking score.

Even when a neural network has extensive historical data, it predicts similarity to a human-defined version of past success. It cannot determine who deserves an opportunity or whether yesterday’s definition of success should govern tomorrow’s institution. Neural networks can make consequential processes more consistent and help reviewers notice overlooked candidates, but accountability for closing a door should remain with an identifiable person operating under transparent policy. Would classmates be more comfortable with an admissions model that is permitted to add applicants to a review pool but prohibited from removing them?
