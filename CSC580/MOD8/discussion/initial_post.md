# Reinforcement Learning for Computer-Chip Floorplanning

A compelling real-world application of reinforcement learning (RL) is computer-chip floorplanning. Before a chip can be manufactured, engineers must determine where to place its major components, including memory blocks, processing units, and control logic, within a limited two-dimensional area. Those components must then be connected while meeting demanding constraints for routing congestion, signal timing, power consumption, performance, and chip area. A poor early placement can create problems that are difficult or impossible to repair later. However, the search space is too large to evaluate every possible arrangement, and traditional floorplanning can require weeks of expert iteration.

Deep RL reframes the task as a sequential decision problem. The environment is the partially completed chip canvas and its underlying circuit graph. The agent observes the components and connections that remain, selects a legal location for the next component, and receives a reward based on the quality of the resulting floorplan. The reward can combine estimated wire length, congestion, density, power, timing, and area while imposing penalties for violations. Because placing one component changes the feasible and useful locations for every later component, the agent must maximize the quality of the complete design rather than greedily choosing the best immediate position.

Mirhoseini et al. (2021) developed a deep-RL method that represents the chip netlist with a graph convolutional neural network and places components sequentially. The researchers reported that the method generated floorplans in less than six hours that were comparable or superior to human-produced layouts across major design metrics. The work was applied to Google accelerator chips, demonstrating that this was more than a simulated board-game problem. Just as importantly, the policy could learn from earlier chip blocks and use that experience when addressing a previously unseen design.

RL is better suited to the decision-making portion of this problem than the other deep-learning methods covered in the course. A convolutional neural network could recognize spatial patterns in an existing layout, but recognizing congestion does not determine the next placement that will produce the strongest final design. A recurrent neural network or long short-term memory model could predict a sequence of placements from historical examples, but supervised training would require labels identifying an optimal action for every partial floorplan. Those labels generally do not exist, and copying historical decisions may reproduce limitations in previous designs. An autoencoder could compress layout features or identify unusual designs, but it would not directly optimize a sequence of actions. RL instead learns from the consequences of its placements and assigns value to decisions whose benefits or costs may not appear until much later.

The approach still requires careful controls. The agent should be trained and evaluated in an electronic-design-automation environment rather than experimenting through costly physical fabrication. Reward design must reflect all important engineering constraints; otherwise, the agent may improve a convenient proxy while producing an impractical layout. Final candidates should pass deterministic design-rule, timing, thermal, power, and manufacturability checks and remain subject to expert review. Performance should also be compared with both human baselines and established optimization tools on held-out chip blocks.

This application illustrates the central distinction between RL and prediction. The objective is not merely to estimate whether a completed floorplan will perform well. The system must choose a series of placements that changes the state of the design and ultimately creates the outcome being evaluated. In that setting, learning a policy for long-term cumulative reward is a natural fit. At the same time, an important governance question remains: when an RL-generated layout meets every formal test but differs substantially from established engineering practice, what evidence should be required before approving it for fabrication?
